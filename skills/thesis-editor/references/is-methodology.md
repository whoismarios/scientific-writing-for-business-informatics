# Methodology in business informatics

Short reference for the methodologist. It does not replace the literature, it helps to ask the right questions of a thesis.

## Design science research

Work that builds and evaluates an artifact is often measured against design science.

**Guidelines by Hevner, March, Park and Ram (2004, MIS Quarterly 28(1), 75–105):**

1. Design as an artifact: the result is a construct, model, method or instantiation.
2. Problem relevance: the artifact solves a relevant problem.
3. Design evaluation: utility, quality and efficacy are demonstrated with suitable methods.
4. Research contributions: a clear contribution to the artifact, foundations or methods.
5. Research rigor: construction and evaluation rely on established methods.
6. Design as a search process: alternatives are considered, decisions justified.
7. Communication: results are understandable for technical and management audiences.

**Process by Peffers, Tuunanen, Rothenberger and Chatterjee (2007, Journal of Management Information Systems 24(3), 45–77):** problem and motivation, objectives of a solution, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, communication.

**Positioning the contribution by Gregor and Hevner (2013, MIS Quarterly 37(2), 337–355):** novelty of problem and solution (improvement, invention, exaptation, routine design). A thesis should state which quadrant it belongs to.

Questions: Does the work say which artifact it produces? Are design decisions justified against alternatives? Is the evaluation adequate to the claim?

## Literature review

- Webster and Watson (2002, MIS Quarterly 26(2), xiii–xxiii): organize literature by concept, not by author. A concept matrix shows which paper covers which concept.
- vom Brocke et al. (2009, ECIS): document the search (sources, search terms, period, forward and backward search) so that it can be reproduced.

Questions: Can the search be repeated? Does the literature lead to a gap, or does it stand unconnected next to the rest?

## Experiments and surveys

**Quality criteria**

| Criterion | Question | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Objectivity | Does the result depend on the person conducting the study? | standardized instructions, automated analysis |
| Reliability | Does the instrument measure consistently? | internal consistency in the own sample, such as Cronbach's alpha or McDonald's omega |
| Internal validity | Can the effect be attributed to the manipulation? | randomization or counterbalancing, holding factors constant, manipulation check |
| External validity | Does the result generalize? | sample, setting, task |
| Construct validity | Does the instrument measure what it should? | established scales, translation process, factor structure |

**Questions for hypothesis tests**

- Was the hypothesis fixed before data collection?
- Does the test fit the design (dependent or independent), scale level and distribution?
- Is the sidedness justified and the correction for multiple tests specified?
- Is an effect size with confidence interval reported next to p?
- Is a non-significant result not read as evidence for the null hypothesis, unless an equivalence test was done?
- Is the power sufficient for the expected effects, and is it reported?

**Instruments**

- source of the instrument and of the translation used
- every adaptation (wording, item selection, scale) named and justified
- reliability values from other samples are not reported as values of the own measurement

## Limitations

A good limitation names the weakness, its cause, its consequence for the conclusions and, where possible, what was done about it. Missing limitations stand out more in reviews than honestly named ones.
